Emotion Regulation Handout 6 (p. 10 of 10) Guilt Words guilt culpability remorse apologetic regret sorry Prompting Events for Feeling Guilt •• Doing or thinking something you believe is wrong. •• Doing or thinking something that violates your personal values. •• Not doing something you said that you would do. •• Committing a transgression against another person or something you value. •• Causing harm/damage to another person or object. •• Causing harm/damage to yourself. •• Being reminded of something wrong you did in the past. •• Other: Interpretations of Events That Prompt Feelings of Guilt •• Thinking that your actions are to blame for something. •• Thinking that you behaved badly. •• Thinking, “If only I had done something differently . . . ” •• Other: Biological Changes and Experiences of Guilt •• Hot, red face. •• Jitteriness, nervousness. •• Suffocating. •• Other: Expressions and Actions of Guilt •• Trying to repair the harm, make amends for the wrongdoing, fix the damage, change the outcome. •• Asking for forgiveness, apologizing, confessing. •• Giving gifts/making sacrifices to try to make up for the transgression. •• Bowing your head; kneeling before the person. Aftereffects of Guilt •• Making resolutions to change. •• Making changes in behavior. •• Joining self-help programs. •• Other: Other Important Emotion Words •• Weariness, dissatisfaction, disinclination. •• Distress. •• Shyness, fragility, reserve, bashfulness, coyness, reticence. •• Cautiousness, reluctance, suspiciousness, caginess, wariness. •• Surprise, amazement, astonishment, awe, startle, wonder. •• Boldness, bravery, courage, determination. •• Powerfulness, a sense of competence, capability, mastery. •• Dubiousness, skepticism, doubtfulness. •• Apathy, boredom, dullness, ennui, fidgetiness, impatience, indifference, listlessness.

OurSpace shared-use support notes

Emotion work starts with the body and access needs. Hunger, pain, oxygen, sleep attacks, MCAS reactions, trauma cues, and exhaustion count.

Tiny steps

  • Name the emotion.
  • Check body basics: breathing, pain, food/water, meds, temperature, sensory load.
  • Lower the demand by one size.
  • Choose soothe, solve, pause, or ask for help.

Flare-day versions

  • Minimum: One breath, one label, one safe message, or one checkbox.
  • Medium: Two to five minutes with everything reachable from bed/chair.
  • Full: Use the original worksheet/handout when both bodies and brains have capacity.

Accessibility modifications

  • Use low-exertion regulation first.
  • Allow anger and grief without turning them into shame.
  • Track patterns without moral judgment.

Relationship and caregiver notes

  • William's needs and Jasper's capacity both count.
  • Ask before helping; ask before escalating; name the smallest next step.
  • Pause means pause, not abandonment. I am coming back. I care about us. I need a smaller next step.
  • What is needed, what capacity exists, what can be smaller, and what can wait?

Avoid or adjust

  • Do not demand immediate calm during respiratory distress, pain flares, dissociation, or overload.
