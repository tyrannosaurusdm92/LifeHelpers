Rief, M.A. (Education) administrative assistant: Alex Viola 3 What do MDs, PhDs, and LCSWs share in common? All of these medical professionals can treat attention deficit disorder (ADHD or ADD) — but which ones provide the treatment services you need, nearby, and at an af- fordable price? Answering those questions — and sorting out the soup of professional acronyms — can get really overwhelming, really fast. Here, we aim to simplify the process with a comparative chart that high- lights the pros and cons of 14 different types of ADHD professionals. Use this to better understand your options and make the right decisions about your family’s mental health care. The good news: Treatment options for attention deficit disorder have multiplied exponentially in the last two decades. The bad news? Choosing the most affordable and effective strategy is overwhelming at best, and downright daunting for many patients and families living with ADHD. Who can prescribe medication? Who can administer diagnostic tests? Who can offer counseling? Who is available for more than 15 minutes at a time? Read on to learn the pros and cons of PCPs, psychologists, counselors, and many other ADHD professionals. By the ADDitude Editors The Professionals Who Diagnose & Treat ADHD: A Comparative Guide 4 Treatment Provider Advantages Disadvantages Family Doctor or Primary Care Physician (PCP) A medical doctor responsible for your overall health care; the first place many people go when they first suspect symptoms of ADHD • Familiar with your or your child’s medical history • Covered by insurance • Can diagnose ADHD and prescribe medication • No referral required; easy to get an appointment • Seldom have in-depth knowledge of ADHD symptoms or treatment • May focus on medication management • Brief office visits may lead to rushed diagnosis and/or treatment Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrician A medical doctor who has additional training in learning disabilities, developmental disorders, and other behavioral challenges • Can provide a more specialized evaluation than most PCPs • Can diagnose and treat related conditions effectively • Often covered by insurance • Can prescribe medication • Appointments may be tough to snag, particularly in underserved areas • May need to refer out for more specialized testing Psychiatrist A medical doctor who specializes in psychiatry; has completed medical school and received education and training in diagnosing and treating mental health disorders like ADHD, depression, and anxiety • Understands the relationship between emotional and physical disorders • Can prescribe medication; may provide talk therapy as well • Adept at diagnosing and treating comorbid conditions • Covered by most insurance plans • May focus on medication management and refer out for talk therapy • Copays often more expensive than are those for PCPs Psychopharmacologist A medical doctor (either an M.D., psychiatrist, or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine) who specializes in the medications used to treat mental and emotional disorders • Often covered by insurance • Adept at prescribing multiple medications for ADHD and comorbid conditions • May serve as a consultant to another specialist, and not meet with you directly 5 Treatment Provider Advantages Disadvantages Psychologist Has an advanced degree in psychology (Ph.D. or Psy.D), and can provide talk therapy to patients in a clinical environment • Can diagnose ADHD and related conditions • Often easier to see than a psychiatrist • Usually covered by insurance • Helps patients manage the emotional challenges of life with ADHD • Can help educate patients about ADHD symptoms and treatment options • In most states, cannot prescribe medication (some exceptions) • Must refer out to a psychiatrist or PCP for medication management Licensed Professional Counselor (LPC) or Licensed Mental Health Counselor (LMHC) Professionals who have been licensed by a state board to provide counseling-based mental therapy • Qualified to diagnose ADHD and most related conditions • Can provide counseling, including marriage and family counseling • Usually less expensive than other mental health professionals • Often covered by insurance • Cannot prescribe medication • May prefer to refer out for more in-depth diagnostic assessments Neurologist A medical doctor who specializes in disorders of the brain, spinal cord, and nervous system • Adept at diagnosing seizure disorders, Tourette Syndrome, or other related brain-based conditions that often appear with ADHD • Can perform brain scans to rule out certain conditions • Can prescribe medication • Not always covered by insurance • May be expensive, particularly when brain scans are performed • EEGs, MRIs, and other brain scans are not necessary to diagnose ADHD itself Neuropsychologist Focuses on a relatively young branch of psychology, and studies how specific structures of the brain relate to psychological disorders and patterns of behavior • Can make recommendations about school placement • Can help children and adults plan out and access multiple treatment interventions • Can fully assess cognitive and behavioral functioning, and provide differential diagnoses • Not usually covered by insurance • May need to refer out for talk therapy, medication management, or other services • Cannot prescribe medication 6 Treatment Provider Advantages Disadvantages Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse (PMHN) A nurse who has undergone additional specialized training to work in psychiatric hospitals and treatment centers • Can assess the mental health needs of individuals, families, and groups • Adept at crisis intervention • Serves as a patient advocate • Often covered by insurance • Most cannot diagnose ADHD or prescribe medication (see APRNs, below) • Usually works in concert with doctors and hospitals, not independently Psychiatric Advanced Practice Registered Nurse (APRN) Psychiatric mental health nurses who have obtained a master’s or doctoral degree in the field • Can diagnose ADHD and related conditions • State laws vary, but can usually prescribe medication and/or provide therapy • Some studies show patient satisfaction on par with primary care doctors • Usually covered by insurance • Depending on state, may have limited prescription abilities or require a physician’s supervision Nurse Practitioner or Psychiatric Nurse Practitioner A type of APRN who has pursued the further education and specialization needed to serve as a primary care provider for children and adults • Usually able to diagnose ADHD and related conditions • Can devise a complete treatment plan and provide therapy • Can prescribe medication • Covered by insurance • Often more available via appointment than are other mental health professionals • In some small studies, NPs outscored physicians in patient satisfaction • Depending on state, may need to collaborate with a physician to prescribe medication • May have limited knowledge of ADHD or related conditions, depending on training 7 Treatment Provider Advantages Disadvantages Licensed Clinical Social Worker (LCSW) A professional who has obtained a Master’s degree (or higher) in social work and has been licensed by a state board to provide mental health services • Able to diagnose ADHD and most related conditions • Excels at providing emotional support to patients and families • Adheres to high ethical standards for patient care • Often costs less than a psychiatrist or psychologist • Usually covered by insurance • Cannot prescribe medication • Depending on training, may struggle with complex diagnoses Occupational Therapist (OT) A licensed professional — usually with a Master’s degree or higher — who helps children and adults with motor deficits, sensory processing challenges, and underdeveloped social skills to function better in day-to-day life • Can diagnose and treat ADHD, SPD, dysgraphia, and many other comorbid conditions • Uses a unique, hands-on therapy that provides motor and sensory benefits • May be accessed through your child’s school • May also offer home visits • Usually more beneficial for children than for adults • Insurance often only covers OT for certain diagnoses • In the U.S., demand exceeds supply for occupational therapy • Cannot prescribe medication ADHD Coach Not a medical professional, but a guide, teacher, and cheerleader who helps teach organizational, time-management, and goal-achieving strategies to teens and adults with ADHD • Helps clients build lifestyle skills (organization, time management, etc.) that are not often covered by medical professionals • Can guide clients toward specific life goals • Many have ADHD — and a unique perspective on the condition • No nationwide standards for licensing coaches currently exist • Can be expensive; rarely covered by OurSpace shared-use support notes

ADHD support should externalize memory, timing, and initiation. The system carries the task so the person does not have to carry shame.

Tiny steps

  • Externalize it: write, tap, speak, or pin it.
  • Shrink it.
  • Add a cue.
  • Add a reward or reset.

Flare-day versions

  • Minimum: One breath, one label, one safe message, or one checkbox.
  • Medium: Two to five minutes with everything reachable from bed/chair.
  • Full: Use the original worksheet/handout when both bodies and brains have capacity.

Accessibility modifications

  • Use visible checklists, defaults, reminders, and one-tap restarts.
  • Assume memory gaps and time blindness need design support.

Relationship and caregiver notes

  • William's needs and Jasper's capacity both count.
  • Ask before helping; ask before escalating; name the smallest next step.
  • Pause means pause, not abandonment. I am coming back. I care about us. I need a smaller next step.
  • What is needed, what capacity exists, what can be smaller, and what can wait?

Avoid or adjust

  • Do not use ADHD tools as character judgments.
