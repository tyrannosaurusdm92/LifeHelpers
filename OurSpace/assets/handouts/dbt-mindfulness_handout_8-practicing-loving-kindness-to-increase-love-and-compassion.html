Mindfulness Handout 8 (Mindfulness Worksheet 6) Practicing Loving Kindness to Increase Love and Compassion What Is Loving Kindness? Loving kindness is a mindfulness practice designed to increase love and compas- sion first for ourselves and then for our loved ones, for friends, for those we are angry with, for difficult people, for enemies, and then for all beings. Loving kindness can protect us from developing and holding on to judgmentalness, ill will, and hostile feelings toward ourselves and others. Practicing Loving Kindness Practicing loving kindness is like saying a prayer for yourself or someone else. As when you are asking or praying for something for yourself or others, you actively send loving and kind wishes, and recite in your mind words and phrases that express good will toward yourself and others. Loving Kindness Instructions 1. Choose a person to send loving kindness toward. Do not select a person you do not want to relate to with kindness and compassion. Start with yourself, or, if this is too difficult, with a person you already love. 2. Sitting, standing, or lying down, begin by breathing slowly and deeply. Opening the palms of your hands, gently bring the person to mind. 3. Radiate loving kindness by reciting a set of warm wishes, such as “May I be happy,” “May I be at peace,” “May I be healthy,” “May I be safe,” or another set of positive wishes of your own. Repeat the script slowly, and focus on the meaning of each word as you say it in your mind. (If you have distracting thoughts, just notice them as they come and go and gently bring your mind back to your script.) Continue until you feel yourself immersed in loving kindness. 4. Gradually work yourself up through loved ones, friends, those you are angry with, difficult people, enemies, and finally all beings. For example, use a script such as “May John be happy,” “May John be at peace,” and so on (or “John, may you be happy,” “May you be at peace,” and so on), as you concentrate on radiating loving kindness to John. 5. Practice each day, starting with yourself and then moving to others.

OurSpace shared-use support notes

Mindfulness is allowed to be tiny, sensory-safe, and done from bed or wheelchair. Noticing without judging counts even when the day is messy.

Tiny steps

  • Notice one body signal.
  • Name one feeling or need.
  • Find one neutral thing in the room.
  • Return to the next safe action.

Flare-day versions

  • Minimum: One breath, one label, one safe message, or one checkbox.
  • Medium: Two to five minutes with everything reachable from bed/chair.
  • Full: Use the original worksheet/handout when both bodies and brains have capacity.

Accessibility modifications

  • Use eyes-open grounding if closing eyes feels unsafe.
  • Use quiet, low-scent, low-touch options for MCAS, trauma, and sensory overload.
  • No posture requirement; lying down or supported sitting is valid.

Relationship and caregiver notes

  • William's needs and Jasper's capacity both count.
  • Ask before helping; ask before escalating; name the smallest next step.
  • Pause means pause, not abandonment. I am coming back. I care about us. I need a smaller next step.
  • What is needed, what capacity exists, what can be smaller, and what can wait?

Avoid or adjust

  • Do not force stillness when it increases pain, panic, dysphoria, or trauma activation.
