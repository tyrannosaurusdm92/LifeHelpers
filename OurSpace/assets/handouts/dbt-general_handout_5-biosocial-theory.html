General Handout 5 (p. 1 of 2) Biosocial Theory Why do I have so much trouble controlling my emotions and my actions? Emotional vulnerability is biological: It’s simply how some people are born.   They are more sensitive to emotional stimuli; they can detect subtle emotional information in the environment that others don’t even notice.   They experience emotions much more often than others.   Their emotions seem to hit for no reason, from out of the blue.   They have more intense emotions.   Their emotions hit like a ton of bricks.   And their emotions are long-lasting. Impulsivity also has a biological basis: Regulating action is harder for some than for others.   They find it very hard to restrain impulsive behaviors.   Often, without thinking, they do things that get them in trouble.   Sometimes their behavior seems to come out of nowhere.   They find it very hard to be effective.   Their moods get in the way of organizing to achieve their goals.   They cannot control behaviors linked to their moods. (continued on next page) General Handout 5 (p. 2 of 2) An invalidating social environment can make it very hard to regulate emotions.   An invalidating environment doesn’t seem to understand your emotions.   It tells you your emotions are invalid, weird, wrong, or bad.   It often ignores your emotional reactions and does nothing to help you.   It may say things like “Don’t be such a baby!” “Quit your blubbering.” “Quit being such a chicken and just solve the problem.” or “Normal people don’t get this frustrated.”   People who invalidate are often doing the best they can.   They may not know how to validate or how important it is to validate, or they may be afraid that if they validate your emotions, you will get more emotional, not less.   They may be under high stress or time pressure, or they may have too few resources themselves.   There may be just a poor fit between you and your social environment: You may be a tulip in a rose garden. An ineffective social environment is a big problem when you want to learn to regulate emotions and actions.   Your environment may reinforce out-of-control emotions and actions.   If people give in when you get out of control, it will be hard for you to get in control.   If others command you to change, but don’t coach you on how to do this, it will be hard to keep on trying to change. It’s the transactions that count between the person and the social environment.   Biology and the social environment influence the person.   The person reciprocates and influences his or her social environment.   The social environment reciprocates and influences the person.   And so on and on and on.

OurSpace shared-use support notes

Use this as a no-blame map. The point is not who failed; the point is what barrier showed up and what support would make the next try smaller.

Tiny steps

  • Name the moment without blame.
  • Circle one link: body, brain, access, trauma cue, timing, or environment.
  • Choose one repair or prevention step.
  • Save the lesson; do not punish the person.

Flare-day versions

  • Minimum: One breath, one label, one safe message, or one checkbox.
  • Medium: Two to five minutes with everything reachable from bed/chair.
  • Full: Use the original worksheet/handout when both bodies and brains have capacity.

Accessibility modifications

  • Do chain analysis verbally, by voice note, or with one checkbox.
  • Include pain, oxygen, MCAS, fatigue, dissociation, caregiver load, and mobility access as real links.

Relationship and caregiver notes

  • William's needs and Jasper's capacity both count.
  • Ask before helping; ask before escalating; name the smallest next step.
  • Pause means pause, not abandonment. I am coming back. I care about us. I need a smaller next step.
  • What is needed, what capacity exists, what can be smaller, and what can wait?

Avoid or adjust

  • Do not use this as a courtroom, interrogation, or proof that someone did not try.
