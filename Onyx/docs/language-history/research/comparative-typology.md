# Five-system comparison: what should be compared

| Language | Broad syllable profile | Sentence tendencies | Main script | Marks and encoding detail |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spanish | (C)(C)V(C), with segmental and dialect restrictions | Neutral SVO, flexible order, frequent null subjects | Latin | ñ, acute accents, ü, inverted punctuation |
| Fijian | Open V/CV; penultimate-mora stress | Verb-initial / often VOS in transitive clauses | Latin-based standard | Unusual letter-to-sound values; few required diacritics |
| Mandarin | (C)(G)V(X), restricted nasal finals; lexical tones | SVO and topic-comment | Han characters; Pinyin auxiliary | Pinyin tone marks and ü; Unicode CJK ideographs |
| Yoruba | Traditional V/CV and syllabic nasals; modern loan strata vary | SVO, preverbal markers, serial verbs | Adapted Latin; Ajami also used | Under-dots plus tone marks; stacked combining sequences |
| Hebrew | Modern broad (C)(C)V(C), earlier stages differ | Biblical clauses often VSO; modern Hebrew commonly SVO | Paleo-Hebrew then square Hebrew | Niqqud, cantillation, final forms and RTL handling |

The formulas are teaching summaries, not parsers. Mandarin characters are not alphabetic letters; Fijian b is not /b/ in the English value; Yoruba dot-below changes letter identity; Hebrew vowel points are later reading marks; Spanish ñ is an alphabet letter. Identical Unicode concepts can serve different linguistic functions. Compare languages at matched levels (ancestor with ancestor, modern norm with modern norm) and name dialect/period for each claim.
