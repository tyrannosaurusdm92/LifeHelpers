# Mandarin Chinese (Standard Mandarin focus): Lineage, root age and sibling branches

**Selection:** Asian / Sinitic language.  
**Lineage:** Sino-Tibetan → Sinitic → Mandarin → Standard Mandarin.

**Root-age record:** Bayesian phylogenetic studies estimate initial Sino-Tibetan divergence at 5,871 BP and 7,184 BP in two 2019 studies; a broader 131-language study estimated a mean root age of 7,983 BP (95% highest posterior density interval 4,778–11,285 BP). BP uses the 1950 baseline. These modeled family ages are not dates for the Sinitic split, Old Chinese writing, or Mandarin. Late Shang oracle-bone records independently attest written Old Chinese in the 13th–11th centuries BCE.

**Evidence type:** Bayesian lexical phylogeny with historical calibrations; separate inscriptional attestation for Old Chinese. **Confidence:** moderate for an ancient family root; calendar estimates and top-level branching depend on sample, cognate coding, calibrations, and model.

**Dating limit:** The models support an ancestor older than 2,500–3,500 years, but they do not date Mandarin or the Sinitic branch directly. Sino-Tibetan internal classification and the Sinitic/non-Sinitic branching model remain debated. Oracle-bone writing dates an artifact record, not spoken language origin.

**Sibling branches / comparison set:** Jin (sometimes grouped with or near Mandarin); Wu; Gan; Xiang; Hakka; Yue; Min; Hui; Pinghua (inventories vary by classification); Tibetic (wider-family comparison); Burmese (wider-family comparison); Karenic (wider-family comparison); Lolo-Burmese and Himalayan languages (wider-family comparisons; subgroup relations are debated).

Mandarin is a Sinitic language in the Sino-Tibetan family. The Sinitic group includes Mandarin and other major branches commonly labeled Jin, Wu, Gan, Xiang, Hakka, Yue, Min, Hui and Pinghua, but subgroup boundaries and counts vary among scholars. Some classifications group Jin with Mandarin; others maintain it separately. These varieties are often called ‘Chinese dialects’ in non-linguistic contexts, but many are not mutually intelligible. Outside Sinitic, Sino-Tibetan includes Tibetic, Burmese, Karenic and many Himalayan languages; proposed higher-level relationships vary.

Three Bayesian lexical studies give different estimates for the first Sino-Tibetan divergence: 5,871 BP and 7,184 BP in two 2019 models, and a mean of 7,983 BP (95% highest posterior density 4,778–11,285 BP) in a broader 2020 sample of 131 languages. All place the family root well beyond 2,500–3,500 years, but these are model-based dates. They depend on which languages and cognates are sampled, the calibration strategy and tree model. They date a proposed root of Sino-Tibetan—not Mandarin, not the Sinitic split as a separately observed event, and not the first written Chinese. Top-level branching remains a debated research question.

Old Chinese refers to an early historical stage before the Qin unification, reconstructed through rhyme patterns, phonetic components in characters, later rhyme dictionaries, dialect evidence, loanwords and comparison. Oracle-bone texts from the Late Shang provide very early direct writing, but a written stage is not a dated proto-language root. Mandarin’s own history is much later: the 1324 Zhongyuan Yinyun is a key Old Mandarin rhyme book, while its phonological system appears to reflect features already established earlier in northern China. Classical written Chinese also had a long history across regions and cannot be equated with one spoken Mandarin lineage.
