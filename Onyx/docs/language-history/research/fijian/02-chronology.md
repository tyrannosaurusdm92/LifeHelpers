# Fijian (standard, Bauan-influenced): Chronology and evidence

**Selection:** Oceanic language.  
**Lineage:** Austronesian → Malayo-Polynesian → Oceanic → Central Pacific → Fijian varieties → Standard Fijian.

Austronesian expansion into Remote Oceania provides an archaeological setting for the Proto-Oceanic calibration. Here BP means years before 1950 CE, the standard archaeological baseline. Researchers infer the language-family chronology from settlement evidence together with linguistic comparison; the roughly 3,000–3,300 BP estimate (BP baseline: 1950 CE) refers to the ancestor’s modeled dispersal, not a Fijian inscription. Later Central Pacific diversification around Fiji produced a network whose descendants include Fijian branches and Polynesian. This is a relative sequence, not an exact list of dated splits.

Written Fijian standardization is much more recent. Missionary activity increased in the late 1830s; John Hunt, James Calvert and Richard Lyth used a printing press and translated scripture. The resulting spelling conventions, with strong Bauan influence, became established through print. The modern standard therefore reflects both Fijian language structure and a specific 19th-century history of literacy and translation. Sound values that appear unusual to English readers were deliberate practical choices, and they should not be “corrected” into English spelling. The code separates prehistoric reconstruction, oral dialect diversity and documented orthographic standardization.
