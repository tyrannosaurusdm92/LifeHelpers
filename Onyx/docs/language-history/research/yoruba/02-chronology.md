# Yoruba (standard orthography; dialect continuum): Chronology and evidence

**Selection:** African language.  
**Lineage:** Niger-Congo (classification contested at highest levels) → Volta-Niger / Benue-Congo groupings vary → Yoruboid → Edekiri / Yoruba varieties.

Yoruba has a long oral history and comparatively recent standardized writing; these evidence streams answer different questions. A Cambridge overview argues that Niger-Congo expansion likely dates to about 12,000 BP, while emphasizing the extensive work still needed to establish the macrofamily’s internal relationships and reconstructions. This is a broad expansion interpretation, not an inscription or a securely fixed ancestor date. At the closer level, the Proto-Yoruboid date in The Yoruba: A New History is a model linking linguistic, archaeological and historical evidence, not an inscription or a universally accepted clock. The Cambridge synthesis cites much wider estimates for separation of neighboring language groups. The repository retains these different time depths and confidence levels instead of selecting a single number as fact.

Latin-based Yoruba writing grew through 19th-century missionary literacy and later standardization. Sources describe 1875-era orthographic activity and a 1974 standard, with later review and continued debate over spelling and tone marking. A 2026 preprint reports a tone-marked speech corpus and concrete NFC/NFD inconsistencies, while another proposes caron/circumflex for contour tones; those marks are proposals, not part of the 1974 standard. Arabic Ajami has also been used in some Muslim communities, without one uniform standard. Standard Yoruba is consequently a social and pedagogical convention that serves readers across dialects. The timeline separates proto-language reconstruction, settlement history, manuscript/missionary practices and official spelling decisions; it does not infer an ancient script from modern diacritics.
