# Hebrew (Biblical through Modern): Sound changes and historical correspondences

**Selection:** Middle Eastern language.  
**Lineage:** Afro-Asiatic → Semitic → West Semitic → Central Semitic → Northwest Semitic → Canaanite → Hebrew.

A major Canaanite development is the shift of earlier Proto-Northwest-Semitic long *ā to *ō in Canaanite, reflected in Hebrew šālōm compared with Arabic salām. Hebrew vowel history includes lengthening, reduction, diphthong contraction and other changes described for Biblical reading traditions. The details of chronology and conditioning are an active scholarly subject; a single comparative example cannot establish that every long a became o in every context. Hebrew also preserves a root-and-pattern morphology in which consonantal roots combine with vocalic and affixal patterns.

Modern Hebrew pronunciations differ from earlier stages in multiple ways. Speakers and traditions show partial or full mergers of historical emphatic stops, loss or merger of pharyngeal and glottal consonants, degemination and loss of some earlier vowel rules. The historical begadkefat alternations and the status of their modern continuations have changed. Modern spoken Hebrew is not an audio recording of Biblical Hebrew, and the Masoretic niqqud system is a later reading tradition. The code records examples by period and tradition and avoids assigning one modern pronunciation to every written ancient form.
