# Hebrew (Biblical through Modern): Lineage, root age and sibling branches

**Selection:** Middle Eastern language.  
**Lineage:** Afro-Asiatic → Semitic → West Semitic → Central Semitic → Northwest Semitic → Canaanite → Hebrew.

**Root-age record:** Canaanite evidence spans roughly 1550–1200 BCE; Hebrew becomes a distinct identifiable language around the Late Bronze / Iron Age transition, not at one exact year.

**Evidence type:** inscriptional evidence plus comparative Semitic reconstruction. **Confidence:** moderate to high for broad classification and period; boundaries are gradual.

**Dating limit:** The date of Canaanite evidence is not the birth date of Proto-Semitic or Modern Hebrew. Neighboring languages form a continuum, and Hebrew developed through ancient, post-biblical and modern stages.

**Sibling branches / comparison set:** Phoenician; Ammonite; Moabite; Edomite; Aramaic (close Northwest Semitic relative, usually classified outside Canaanite); Ugaritic (Late Bronze Northwest Semitic; some references include it within Canaanite, while others draw a narrower boundary).

Hebrew is Afro-Asiatic → Semitic → West Semitic → Central Semitic → Northwest Semitic → Canaanite. Within Canaanite it is compared with Phoenician and the Transjordanian languages Ammonite, Moabite and Edomite. Aramaic is a close Northwest Semitic relative but is distinguished from Canaanite in standard classifications. Ugaritic is a Late Bronze Northwest Semitic language; reference works differ on whether its dialect belongs inside the Canaanite subgroup or outside a narrower Canaanite node. This profile keeps that placement visible as a classification caveat rather than presenting one tree as uncontested.

Canaanite dialects are attested in the Late Bronze Age, around 1550–1200 BCE. The Cambridge account cautions that Hebrew probably did not emerge as a discrete language before the Late Bronze / Early Iron transition, even though some Late Bronze Canaanite features anticipate Hebrew. Ancient Hebrew later has Archaic, Biblical, Second Temple and post-biblical stages; Modern Hebrew was revived and standardized as a spoken language much later. The lineage map connects these stages without implying that a single unchanged Hebrew community or pronunciation persisted throughout. The ancestor dates qualify the family-depth request; they are not dates for modern Israeli Hebrew.
