## Script, Unicode, diacritics, and glyphs

The Aramaic consonantal alphabet is written right-to-left. Old Aramaic inscriptional forms gave way to a widely used Imperial/official chancery hand; after imperial standardization weakened, regional cursive and monumental styles diverged. The tradition became a source for Jewish square script, Palmyrene, Syriac, Mandaic, and Nabataean; Nabataean script development is important to Arabic script history. Descendant scripts are related but not interchangeable: ductus, ligatures, letterforms, and later vowel pointing differ by community and period.

**Unicode model:** Imperial Aramaic U+10840–U+1085F is encoded separately from Hebrew, Syriac, Nabataean, Palmyrene, and other related scripts. Text direction is right-to-left.

|---|---|---|
| 𐡀 | U+10840 | Imperial Aramaic Aleph |
| 𐡁 | U+10841 | Imperial Aramaic Beth |
| 𐡂 | U+10842 | Imperial Aramaic Gimel |
| 𐡃 | U+10843 | Imperial Aramaic Daleth |

**Subscripts:** No productive subscript alphabet in Imperial Aramaic. Scholarly transliteration may use subscript numerals or dots as editorial notation; those are not Aramaic script characters.

**Diacritics and combining marks:** Early abjads generally leave short vowels unwritten; later script traditions developed distinct pointing systems. These vowel marks are tradition-specific and must not be projected backward onto Imperial Aramaic. Combining marks in later Syriac/Hebrew text have their own Unicode blocks and ordering rules.

**Glyph strokes and layout:** Unicode encodes character identity and text order, not ductus. Paleographic letterforms evolve through inscriptions and manuscripts; code point identity does not prescribe historical stroke order.

Unicode encodes characters, not the complete historical calligraphic or palaeographic process. Record source form, normalization, transliteration, and rendering assumptions separately.
